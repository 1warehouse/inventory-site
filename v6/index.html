Have you got bicarb?

You’re in aisle four. Someone texts and asks. You genuinely do not know. There’s a picture in your head of a tub at the back of the larder, but you had that picture last time too, and last time you were wrong, and that is why there are now two.

That is the whole job. Not stock control. Not asset management. Being able to answer a question about your own house from somewhere that isn’t your house.

So you open the app in the shop and type b-i-c. The answer arrives before you finish the word, because the search runs on the list already in your hand. It does not go off and ask a server.

Now the honest half, because the scanner is the reason people install this and the reason some of them uninstall it. Shop-bought things with a printed retail barcode come back with a name and a picture: tins, jars, cleaning stuff, medicine, books with an ISBN, board games, Lego boxes, DVDs, batteries. Your own asset labels, internal SKUs and a lot of own-brand corner-shop stock will scan perfectly well and come back blank, and then you type the name yourself. Loose fixings, pre-barcode books, most clothing once it’s out of the packet and anything handmade is manual entry, always.

And scanning is not the same thing as looking up. Pointing the camera at a barcode is free and unmetered forever, on both plans, with no counter anywhere. Asking the global database what that barcode is costs you one of five lookups a day on the free plan, or one of two hundred on Premium. Two minutes into a serious first session on a full larder you will hit five. We would rather you knew that here than found it out there.

“I’ll start it, and then I’ll stop.”

You’re right to think that. Everybody who has ever downloaded an inventory app has abandoned one. The list gets built in an enthusiastic weekend and then it quietly stops being true, and a list that isn’t true is worse than no list, because you trust it once and get burned.

So here is the only mechanism that matters, and it is less a feature than a shape. The second time you scan something, you are not adding it. The camera pauses, the product you already have comes up with a big editable number and a plus and a minus, you thumb it from 3 down to 1, and the camera comes back. Walk the shelf. Scan, thumb, scan, thumb. Recounting is the same loop you used to build the thing in the first place, which is why it actually gets done.

The other half is that a date on a product does work while you are not using the app at all, which is a few sections down.

And here is where the fear is justified. Nothing decrements on its own. There is no till, no sales feed, no integration reaching in to adjust a number when something leaves the house. If you sell things for a living, this count will be wrong inside a week and you should buy something else. If you keep things, it will be as accurate as the last time you walked past a shelf with your phone in your hand.

It’s a Google Sheet. In your Drive. With your name on it.

Stop reading for ten seconds and do this bit yourself, because it is the one claim on this page you can check without installing anything. Open drive.google.com on a laptop. Now picture a file called My Inventory sitting in it. That isn’t a metaphor for where we keep your data. That is where your data is.

When you make an inventory, the app creates an actual Google Spreadsheet in your Drive and a folder next to it for the photos. Every product you add is a row written into that file. Every list you look at is read back out of it. Our own database holds accounts, subscriptions, push tokens, support tickets and the definitions of your fields and locations. No table in it holds your inventory: no quantity, no location value, no custom field value, and none of your photos. The only product data we keep at all is a shared cache of public barcode lookups, identical for every user, so that the second person to scan a tin of passata gets the answer instantly.

The file has four tabs.

INFO

Written when the sheet is created, in your language. A welcome line, a link back to us, two house rules (don’t change the column names or their positions, and don’t rename or delete the tabs) and a link straight to the Drive folder where your photos live, which is the only place in the whole product that tells you where they are.

PRODUCTS

Seven columns before you add any of your own, in this order: A Name, B Quantity, C Barcode, D Location, E Description, F Image, G Last Updated. The header row arrives bold, centred and frozen. A filter is already applied across A to G. Quantity is number-formatted as a whole number.

HISTORY

Every product created, every product deleted, every quantity changed, with the name of the person who did it and when. Premium only, and it starts the day you subscribe. There is no backfill and we can’t invent one, because we never had the data.

ALERTS

A log of every reminder that has fired: the product, the field, the value that triggered it, and which rule caught it.

You can sort the Products tab, pivot it, chart it, print it as a stocktake or send it to your accountant, and none of that involves us in any way.

And one detail that will matter to roughly one reader in five, and matter a great deal to that one. When the app writes to your sheet it updates the value and the formatting, and nothing else. That is a deliberate choice with a comment next to it in the code explaining why: writing everything would also wipe your notes and your data validation. So you can carry on using the file as a spreadsheet. Add a column by hand and the app reads it back as one of your fields. Drag a column somewhere else and the app follows it — dragging columns in Google Sheets is in fact the only way to reorder your fields, because there are no drag handles in the app at all.

There is no 1inventory web app and there never has been. The desktop half of this product is Google Sheets. That is the entire desktop story, and we say so plainly instead of letting the word “anywhere” do the lying for us.

Your old list, already in, before the kettle’s boiled

If you have kept a spreadsheet for six years, you are not retyping it, and we are not going to ask you to. Export it as a CSV, upload it, and it’s in.

The clever part is that your headers get matched by meaning instead of by exact text. A file with Bezeichnung, Menge and Lagerort across the top lands on Name, Quantity and Location without you touching anything. Fields and locations you don’t have yet are created as the file is read.

What comes back is a receipt: how many rows landed, how many were skipped and which reason applied, and how every single column in your file was matched, labelled Standard, Custom, New or Ignored.

One thing here we are quietly pleased with. If your file has more columns than your plan allows fields, we don’t drop them. They get written into your Google Sheet anyway and marked Saved to Google Sheets only, with a line telling you that adding a custom field of the same name later will link it up automatically. Nothing in your file is thrown away because of what you pay.

Four things to know first, because there is no undo

  • CSV only. If it’s an .xlsx, Save As first.
  • There is no mapping screen. The matching happens in one pass and you see the report afterwards. There is no preview beforehand.
  • It only ever inserts. Anything already in your inventory by name or by barcode is skipped and never overwritten, so you can run the same file twice safely, and a corrected file will not apply its corrections.
  • 5 MB, 60 columns, and one of them has to be a product name.

The Calpol in the bathroom cupboard went out of date in April.

Nobody knew. It sat there being a plan for a bad night. Then at half past two on a Tuesday it turned out to be a bottle of pink liquid nine months past its date, and the nearest open pharmacy was eleven miles away.

That is the one thing a list on its own can’t do for you. A spreadsheet will hold that date perfectly well. It just won’t ever mention it again.

So: put a date on a product, tell the app how much warning you want, and it gets in touch.

What people put dates on

  • Food and medicine with a date printed on the box
  • Warranties worth claiming
  • An MOT
  • A boiler service
  • A filter change
  • The insurance that auto-renews at a worse price if nobody looks

How the rule behaves

  • Push, email, or both
  • Seven days of warning by default, anything from the same day out to a year
  • Before the date, or after it, for the things that slip
  • At an hour of the day you choose
  • Pointed at particular locations, if you only care about the bathroom cupboard
  • Twelve things falling due at once gets you one message about twelve things, which is the difference between a feature people keep switched on and one they mute in week two

It watches dates. It cannot watch quantities. There is no low-stock alert, no reorder point, no par level and nothing anywhere in the product that knows or cares that you are running out of something. If that is what you came here for, it isn’t here and it isn’t coming next month.

One alert rule is on the free plan, with both push and email working properly on it. Fifty on Premium. The catch, since we are listing catches: a rule watches a date field you created, and the free plan gives you one custom field, so on Free that one field has to be the date.

Supplier isn’t one of our fields. It’s one of yours.

The product record is deliberately thin. A name, a number, a barcode, a location, a description, one photo. Everything else is a column you add, and it appears in your spreadsheet as column H, then I, then J.

Each type arrives in the sheet correctly formatted, which is the part that matters and the part a hand-built spreadsheet always gets wrong by month three. A Decimal column comes with a 0.00 format on it, so you can sum a column of prices. A Date column comes formatted as a date, so you can sort by it. A Checkbox becomes an actual Sheets tick box instead of the word TRUE in eleven hundred cells.

What people actually add. A collector: Edition, Condition, Bought from. A workshop: Thread size, Last serviced. A holiday let: Replaced on. Fifteen fields on Premium, one on Free.

Larder, bathroom cupboard, garage, loft, van.

That is five, and five is the shape of a house. It is also the shape of a holiday let, a club kit store, a two-person craft business, a church hall and a band’s gear.

Locations are flat. There is no Building inside Room inside Shelf, and if you need aisles A to Z or fifty numbered moving boxes, this isn’t the product. What you get is a list of named places, a folder view with a count on each folder, empty folders included so you can build the structure before you own anything, and a permanent Without location folder for the things you haven’t decided about. You are never stopped from saving something by a question you don’t feel like answering.

Swipe a folder right to rename it, left to delete it. Deleting a place doesn’t delete what is in it unless you tick the box that says so, and that box starts unticked. The list itself sorts by whatever you like: last updated, name, or quantity, with pictures on or off, and up to two extra fields shown on every row, so a pantry can see best-before dates and a collector can see catalogue numbers.

Things it doesn’t do, listed properly.

You’ll normally find this list somewhere between nowhere and the eleventh FAQ. Here it is, fifth from the bottom of the homepage, because the most valuable thing we own is a stranger’s benefit of the doubt, and a paragraph costs less than a refund.

  • No offline mode. Every action is a network call. A cellar, a lock-up, a van in an underground car park and a field with a market stall in it are all places where this app will not work. It’s the biggest gap in the product and we know it.
  • No export button. Covered above, and we think it’s the right answer, but if what you wanted was a PDF stock report to hand somebody, there isn’t one.
  • No low-stock alerts, no reorder points, no suppliers, no purchase orders, no prices, no SKUs, no categories, no units of measure, no sales and no transactions of any kind.
  • No iPad layout, no web app, no desktop app. Portrait phone only, on both platforms. Your data opens on a laptop in Google Sheets, and that is the entire desktop story.
  • One photo per product, compressed hard. This is not the app for documenting a house for an insurer, and if that is the job, please go and buy something built for it.
  • No nested locations, no bulk edit, no multi-select, no grid view, no barcode label printing, no decimal quantities.
  • Google sign-in only. There is no email and password, and there is no local-only mode. If you don’t want a Google account attached to this, there isn’t a version of this product for you.
  • Up to 10,000 products per inventory, on both plans. You will almost certainly never reach it. It is a real number, so we print it.
  • Five languages: English, German, Spanish, French and Portuguese. If yours isn’t one of them, the app will be in English.

Read the permission screen before you tap Allow. We’ll wait.

The app asks Google for three things: your name, your email address, and a permission called drive.file. That is the narrowest Drive permission that exists. It means an app can only touch files it made itself.

What it can do

  • Create your inventory spreadsheet
  • Create a folder for its photos
  • Read and edit those two things
  • Put photos in that folder
  • List the revision history of that one file

What it cannot do

  • See any other file in your Drive
  • Search your Drive
  • Open a document you made
  • Read your Gmail
  • Read your Calendar or your Contacts
  • Notice that any of it is there at all

The broad permissions that would allow any of that are not requested, so they cannot be used. Google enforces this. We don’t. And it takes about ten seconds to check: it is printed on the consent screen when you sign in, and it is there afterwards under Google Account › Security › Third-party apps, next to the button that revokes it.

One claim we won’t make. Your rows travel through our servers on the way to and from your sheet, so the honest sentence is that we don’t store your data. It is not that we can’t see it. Anyone selling you the second sentence about a product built this way is describing a different product.

If you stop paying

If you delete your 1inventory account

Same answer. There is a checkbox that also wipes the sheets from your Drive, and it starts unticked. You would have to go looking for it.

If we are gone

Sparkybit is a small company in Paderborn and we are not going to pretend that is the same thing as a guarantee. So here is the guarantee that doesn’t depend on us being here. Tomorrow morning your spreadsheet is exactly where it was tonight, with every product, every count, every column you added and every change anybody logged, in the most portable format there is, readable by software owned by somebody else entirely. What you would lose is the scanner, the lookup and the folder view. Your data survives. You do lose something, and we say which. We’d rather compete on the footing where you can leave.

What the app says when it falls over

If it ever crashes, it doesn’t show you a stack trace. It shows you a sentence: “Don’t worry, your data is safe in your Google Sheets.”

Plans

Free is a real plan, not a trial.

You go Premium when one of five things becomes true. You need more than one inventory. More than one place. More than one person. More than one date reminder. Or you want a record of who changed what.

Until one of those happens, the free plan isn’t a countdown. It is a permanent, working, single-person catalogue with unlimited barcode scanning, up to 10,000 products, CSV import, and one live alert rule with both email and push.

What the Premium numbers actually buy:

  • Five inventories. The pantry, the workshop, the books, the band’s gear and the holiday let, each with its own spreadsheet, its own fields and its own icon from a set of 88.
  • Five locations. The garage, the loft, the shed, the stockroom and the van, each with its own folder and its own count.
  • Five people. You and four others, each with their own sign-in and their own name against every change they make.

Where are the five-star reviews?

There are no star ratings on this page, no logos, no quotes from delighted users, and no “trusted by 40,000 households”. The app is new, we have none of those things, and inventing them would be the single stupidest move available to a company whose entire argument is that you can verify what we tell you.

So here is the substitute, and we’d argue it is the better currency anyway. Almost everything on this page can be checked in under a minute, most of it before you install anything.

  • The permission. Read the Google consent screen when you sign in. It will say drive.file, and it will not say anything else.
  • The file. Open drive.google.com after your first scan. The spreadsheet will be sitting there under your own account, created about four seconds ago.
