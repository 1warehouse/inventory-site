Barcode inventory app · iPhone and Android · Built by Sparkybit GmbH, Paderborn

Three taps and the tin’s in your spreadsheet. That’s how you stop buying a fourth one.

1inventory is a barcode scanner for a Google Sheet you own. Point the camera at anything with a printed barcode and the name, the description and the photo fill themselves in. The spreadsheet it writes to is a real one, in your Drive, with your name on it. You can open it on a laptop while you’re still standing in the kitchen.

Free to install. One tap to sign in with Google. No form, no password, nothing to verify by email. Your first inventory exists before you reach the home screen.

Have you got bicarb?

You’re in aisle four. Someone texts and asks. You genuinely do not know. There’s a picture in your head of a tub at the back of the larder, but you had that picture last time too, and last time you were wrong, and that’s why there are now two.

That is the whole job. Not stock control. Not asset management. Just being able to answer a question about your own house from somewhere that isn’t your house.

So you open the app in the shop and type b-i-c. The answer arrives before you finish the word, because the search runs on the list already in your hand rather than going off to ask a server.

C

Three taps per thing. Here they are.

One. Tap the big green circle in the middle of the bottom bar. The camera opens with a scan window in the middle and everything else dimmed. Four corner brackets sit around it in white, and they flip to green the instant they catch a code. It zooms itself in on small print, there’s a torch for the back of a dark cupboard, and it will read twelve formats.

Two. If it’s something you already own, the quantity comes straight up and you’re done. If it’s new, the app says Not in your inventory, then Searching global database for product information, and hands you a create form with the name, the description and a photo already in it. If your phone is set to German, the name arrives in German, even if the product is French.

Three. Hit Save & scan next. The camera is back up before your hand has moved.

Most inventory apps ask you to type. This one asks you to point.

Now the honest half.

Shop-bought things with a printed retail barcode come back with a name and a picture: tins, jars, cleaning stuff, medicine, books with an ISBN, board games, Lego boxes, DVDs, batteries. Your own asset labels, internal SKUs and a lot of own-brand corner-shop stock will scan perfectly well and come back blank, and then you type the name yourself. Loose fixings, pre-barcode books, most clothing once it’s out of the packet and anything handmade is manual entry, always.

And scanning is not the same thing as looking up. Pointing the camera at a barcode is free and unmetered forever, on both plans, with no counter anywhere. Asking the global database what that barcode is costs you one of 5 lookups a day on the free plan, or one of 200 on Premium. Two minutes into a serious first session on a full larder you will hit five. We would rather you knew that here than found it out there.

D

One shelf. Forty-one rows. About an hour.

Here is that larder after an afternoon, and it is not tidy.

There’s a row in capitals because that’s how the jar was labelled and nobody went back to fix it. A row with no barcode, because the bag came from a market stall. A row with a quantity of zero, because the bicarb ran out in February and nobody has been to a shop since. And a description field that just says for cake??.

That’s what a working inventory looks like. The app doesn’t ask you to be neat and it doesn’t tidy up behind you. Rows with no name get skipped rather than becoming ghost products. Blank spacer rows are ignored. Anything you type into a column the app has never heard of comes back as one of your own fields instead of being thrown away.

Filling the list is one afternoon. Counting it is the rest of the year. The rest of this page is about the year.

E

“I’ll start it, and then I’ll stop.”

You’re right to think that. Everybody who has ever downloaded an inventory app has abandoned one. The list gets built in an enthusiastic weekend and then it quietly stops being true, and a list that isn’t true is worse than no list, because you trust it once and get burned.

So here is the only mechanism that matters, and it’s less a feature than a shape. The second time you scan something, you’re not adding it. The camera pauses, the product you already have comes up with a big editable number and a plus and a minus, you thumb it from 3 down to 1, and the camera comes back. Walk the shelf. Scan, thumb, scan, thumb. Recounting is the same loop you used to build the thing in the first place, which is why it actually gets done.

The other half is that a date on a product does work while you’re not using the app at all, which is a few sections down.

And here’s where the fear is justified. Nothing decrements on its own. There is no till, no sales feed, no integration reaching in to adjust a number when something leaves the house. If you sell things for a living, this count will be wrong inside a week and you should buy something else. If you keep things, it’ll be as accurate as the last time you walked past a shelf with your phone in your hand.

F
The claim you can check without installing anything

It’s a Google Sheet. In your Drive. With your name on it.

Stop reading for ten seconds and do this bit yourself, because it’s the one claim on this page you can check without installing anything. Open drive.google.com on a laptop. Now picture a file called My Inventory sitting in it. That isn’t a metaphor for where we keep your data. That is where your data is.

When you make an inventory, the app creates an actual Google Spreadsheet in your Drive and a folder next to it for the photos. Every product you add is a row written into that file. Every list you look at is read back out of it. Our own database holds accounts, subscriptions, push tokens, support tickets and the definitions of your fields and locations. No table in it holds your inventory — not a quantity, not a location value, not a custom field value, and none of your photos. The only product data we keep at all is a shared cache of public barcode lookups, identical for every user, so that the second person to scan a tin of passata gets the answer instantly.

The file has four tabs.

  • INFO. Written when the sheet is created, in your language. A welcome line, a link back to us, two house rules, and a link straight to the Drive folder where your photos live — the only place in the whole product that tells you where they are.
  • PRODUCTS. Seven columns before you add any of your own: A Name, B Quantity, C Barcode, D Location, E Description, F Image, G Last Updated. Header row bold, centred and frozen, with a filter already applied across A to G.
  • HISTORY. Every product created, every product deleted, every quantity changed, with who did it and when. Premium only, and it starts the day you subscribe. There is no backfill and we can’t invent one, because we never had the data.
  • ALERTS. A log of every reminder that has fired: the product, the field, the value that triggered it, and which rule caught it.

And one detail that will matter to roughly one reader in five, and matter a great deal to that one. When the app writes to your sheet it updates the value and the formatting, and nothing else. That’s a deliberate choice with a comment next to it in the code explaining why: writing everything would also wipe your notes and your data validation. So you can carry on using the file as a spreadsheet. Add a column by hand and the app reads it back as one of your fields. Drag a column somewhere else and the app follows it — dragging columns in Google Sheets is in fact the only way to reorder your fields, because there are no drag handles in the app at all.

There is no 1inventory web app and there never has been. The desktop half of this product is Google Sheets, and we’d rather say that plainly than let the word “anywhere” do the lying for us.

There’s no export button, because there’s nothing to export.
